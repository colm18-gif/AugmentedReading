Evidence · the open question

What we know, and what we don't.

The honest answer to "is there evidence this works" is: partly, and not the part that matters most. Here is the whole of it.

01

Established

Visuals alongside text aid comprehension.

This is well supported and not controversial. Readers given well-chosen visuals alongside prose understand and retain more than readers given prose alone (Mayer and Gallini, 1990; Carney and Levin, 2002), an effect confirmed across a large body of studies by meta-analysis (Guo et al., 2020).

Every claim on this site about the value of pictures rests on that literature, and nothing more.

02

Untested

No efficacy data exists yet

Whether an inline generated visual delivers that same gain.

Nobody has answered this — including me. The literature above concerns visuals that already exist and were chosen by an author or an editor. Whether a visual generated in response to a reader's selection produces the same comprehension gain is a different question, and it is open.

Two recent studies bear on it and are worth stating plainly rather than quietly. A controlled investigation of AI help anchored to text found that readers preferred help integrated with the document, and preferred choosing the passage themselves — but found no measurable effect on comprehension (Joshi and Vogel, 2026). Its help was textual, which leaves the visual question untouched. Separately, an interface that links readers to figures already present in a document improved reading-quiz scores without adding time or cognitive load (Hwang et al., 2026) — though it surfaces visuals the document already contains rather than producing one where the prose has none.

03

The study

How the question gets settled.

The design is not elaborate. Readers work through a genuinely difficult passage under three conditions, and comprehension is measured with questions that test understanding and transfer rather than recall.

A

Unaided — the passage alone

B

Free to leave the page and search the web for a supporting image

C

Reading with an inline comprehension tool

The comparison that matters is B against C. Leaving the page already delivers some benefit, so the tool has to beat the reader's existing workaround — not merely beat nothing. The passage also has to be hard enough that there is room to improve.

04

Position

A negative result would be worth having.

The answer might be no. A tool can be fast, well-liked and frequently used and still make no difference to understanding — or substitute a plausible-looking picture for genuine comprehension. If that is what the evidence shows, it is worth knowing before anyone builds on it at scale.

The value is in having an answer, not in a particular answer. That is why the study is described here in advance of running it, rather than after.
